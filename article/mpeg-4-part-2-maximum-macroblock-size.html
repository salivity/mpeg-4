MPEG-4 Part 2 Maximum Macroblock Size

This article provides an overview of the macroblock architecture defined in baseline MPEG-4 Part 2 video compression. It specifically examines the maximum supported macroblock dimensions, explains how these processing blocks are structured across luma and chroma components, and details why this fixed block size is central to the standard's motion compensation and transform coding.

In baseline MPEG-4 Part 2 (ISO/IEC 14496-2) video compression, the maximum macroblock size is strictly 16x16 pixels.

Unlike modern video codecs such as HEVC (H.265) or AV1, which allow variable block sizes up to 64x64 or 128x128 pixels, MPEG-4 Part 2 relies on a fixed macroblock framework inherited from earlier standards like MPEG-1, MPEG-2, and H.263.

Macroblock Structure and Composition

A standard 16x16 macroblock in MPEG-4 Part 2 represents a square region of 16x16 luma (brightness) samples. In the standard YCbCr 4:2:0 chroma subsampling format, this block consists of:

  • Four 8x8 Luminance (Y) Blocks: Covering the full 16x16 pixel area.
  • One 8x8 Chrominance Blue (Cb) Block: Subsampled spatially by half in both horizontal and vertical directions.
  • One 8x8 Chrominance Red (Cr) Block: Subsampled spatially by half in both horizontal and vertical directions.

Together, these six 8x8 sub-blocks make up the complete 16x16 macroblock.

Partitioning and Transform Sizes

While 16x16 is the maximum macroblock size, MPEG-4 Part 2 allows specific sub-partitions for motion estimation and compensation:

  • 16x16 Motion Compensation: A single motion vector applies to the entire 16x16 luma area.
  • 8x8 Motion Compensation: Advanced Prediction modes allow the macroblock to be split into four independent 8x8 blocks, each having its own motion vector.

Regardless of whether 16x16 or 8x8 motion vectors are utilized, the Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) and quantization processes always operate on fundamental 8x8 sample blocks. The macroblock container itself never exceeds 16x16 pixels.
