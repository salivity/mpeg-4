Why Early Video Security Used MPEG-4 Over MJPEG

Early digital video security systems transitioned from Motion JPEG (M-JPEG) to MPEG-4 Part 2 primarily because MPEG-4 dramatically reduced storage requirements and network bandwidth consumption. While M-JPEG simply compressed individual video frames as separate JPEG images, MPEG-4 Part 2 introduced efficient temporal compression. This shift allowed surveillance systems to stream smoother video over limited networks, store significantly more footage on cost-prohibitive hard drives, and enable scalable remote monitoring without overloading early IT infrastructure.

The Limitation of Motion JPEG: Intra-Frame Compression

Motion JPEG functions by capturing a sequence of individual, full-frame JPEG images. Because every frame is compressed independently—a method known as intra-frame compression—the codec does not consider the relationship between consecutive frames.

In typical surveillance environments, large portions of the scene (such as walls, hallways, or parking lots) remain static from one second to the next. M-JPEG redundantly encoded these unchanged background elements in every single frame, resulting in massive file sizes and excessively high bitrates, regardless of whether activity was occurring in the frame.

The Advantage of MPEG-4 Part 2: Inter-Frame Compression

MPEG-4 Part 2 introduced inter-frame compression to the video surveillance market. Instead of saving every frame as a standalone picture, it uses three primary frame types:

  • I-Frames (Intra-coded): Complete images serving as reference points.
  • P-Frames (Predicted): Compressed frames that only record changes that occurred since the previous frame.
  • B-Frames (Bi-directional): Frames that reference changes from both preceding and following frames.

Because the system only transmitted and stored pixel changes rather than entire new scenes, MPEG-4 Part 2 could achieve the same perceived visual quality as M-JPEG at a fraction of the data rate.

Storage Capacity and Cost Savings

During the late 1990s and early 2000s, hard drive storage was expensive and capacities were limited. Security operations often required weeks or months of continuous recording. Using M-JPEG, a standard multi-camera digital video recorder (DVR) would rapidly exhaust available storage, forcing operators to lower the frame rate (often to 1–5 frames per second) or reduce image resolution. MPEG-4 Part 2 allowed systems to record at higher, smoother frame rates while extending retention windows without increasing hardware costs.

Network Bandwidth and Remote Monitoring

The emergence of early IP cameras and networked video recorders (NVRs) coincided with early broadband and local area networks, which had limited throughput. M-JPEG streams frequently saturated 10/100 Mbps local networks and were nearly impossible to stream over early WAN or dial-up/DSL connections.

MPEG-4 Part 2’s lower bitrate profile allowed multiple camera feeds to coexist on standard corporate networks and made remote live monitoring via web browsers or client software feasible.

Computational Feasibility Before H.264

While more advanced standards like H.264 (MPEG-4 Part 10) eventually became the industry standard, early embedded digital signal processors (DSPs) lacked the processing power to encode H.264 in real time at a reasonable cost. MPEG-4 Part 2 hit the ideal balance between computational complexity and compression efficiency, making it the preferred standard for the first major generation of modern digital surveillance systems.
