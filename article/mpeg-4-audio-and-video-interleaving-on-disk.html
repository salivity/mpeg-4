MPEG-4 Audio and Video Interleaving on Disk

The MPEG-4 standard manages the interleaving of audio and video chunks on disk through a flexible metadata structure rather than enforcing a rigid numerical ratio. Instead of mandating fixed chunk sizes or strict sample-to-sample ratios, the standard defines an indexing architecture within the ISO Base Media File Format (MPEG-4 Part 14) that allows multiplexers to arrange data for optimal storage, buffering, and sequential playback performance.

Specification vs. Implementation Discretion

The MPEG-4 specification (ISO/IEC 14496-12 and ISO/IEC 14496-14) intentionally avoids prescribing a mandatory interleaving ratio. The standard defines how to describe the interleaved layout rather than how often tracks must alternate. The exact interleaving strategy is left to the multiplexer (muxer), which tailors the layout to the target medium, playback buffer size, and transport constraints.

The Chunk and Sample Indexing Mechanism

MPEG-4 handles media storage by dividing audio and video samples into contiguous blocks called "chunks" inside the media data (mdat) container. The relationship between these chunks and their location on disk is regulated by the metadata stored in the movie box (moov):

  • Sample-to-Chunk Box (stsc): Defines how many media samples (e.g., individual video frames or audio access units) are grouped into each chunk.
  • Chunk Offset Box (stco or co64): Stores the exact byte offsets on disk for the beginning of each chunk, supporting 32-bit and 64-bit address spaces.
  • Time-to-Sample Box (stts): Maps playback duration to samples, allowing players to synchronize tracks regardless of their physical distribution on disk.

Because chunks are referenced by explicit file offsets, the file format supports arbitrary interleaving patterns, ranging from single-sample alternation to large contiguous blocks of multi-second media.

Standard Best Practices for Interleaving

While the standard allows flexibility, real-world MPEG-4 implementations rely on standard interleaving guidelines to minimize disk seek times and prevent buffer underflows:

  • Time-Based Granularity: Most encoders interleave audio and video based on playback duration rather than byte count, commonly grouping roughly 0.5 to 1.0 seconds of media per chunk.
  • Buffer Management: Audio chunks are typically multiplexed slightly ahead of their corresponding video frames on disk to ensure audio decoders have sufficient lead time to fill buffers before playback begins.
  • Sequential Read Optimization: Grouping related temporal data closely together reduces mechanical drive head movement (or read-ahead cache misses on solid-state drives), enabling steady, linear read operations during playback.
