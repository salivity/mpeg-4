MPEG-4 Sub-Pixel Motion Estimation and 6-Tap Filtering

Sub-pixel motion estimation is a critical component in modern video compression standards, such as MPEG-4 Part 10 (AVC/H.264), designed to enhance inter-frame prediction beyond integer-pixel accuracy. To capture fractional movement smoothly without introducing significant blur or aliasing, the codec utilizes a 6-tap Finite Impulse Response (FIR) Wiener filter to interpolate half-pixel sample values from surrounding integer pixels. This article explains how the 6-tap filter functions within the sub-pixel motion estimation pipeline, detail its mathematical application across horizontal, vertical, and diagonal planes, and outlines how it enables quarter-pixel precision to minimize prediction error.

The Role of Sub-Pixel Accuracy

In real-world video capture, moving objects and camera motions rarely align perfectly with the discrete boundaries of the pixel grid. Restricting motion vectors to full-integer positions leads to higher residual energy—the difference between the predicted block and the actual block—which requires more bits to encode. Sub-pixel motion estimation allows motion vectors to point to fractional locations (half-pel and quarter-pel positions), significantly lowering prediction error and improving overall compression efficiency.

The 6-Tap Wiener Interpolation Filter

Standard linear interpolation (such as bilinear filtering) often introduces low-pass filtering artifacts, causing noticeable blurring in reconstructed video frames. To preserve high-frequency details and edge sharpness, MPEG-4 employs a symmetric 6-tap FIR filter based on Wiener filter design principles.

The filter uses the specific weight vector:

\[\frac{1}{32} \times [1, -5, 20, 20, -5, 1]\]

This configuration acts as an approximation of an ideal sinc interpolation filter, optimized to minimize mean squared error (MSE) while maintaining low computational complexity via shifts and additions (division by 32 equates to a 5-bit right shift).

Step-by-Step Interpolation Process

The sub-pixel motion estimation workflow using the 6-tap filter is carried out in sequential stages:

1. Half-Pixel Interpolation (Horizontal and Vertical)

To compute a half-pixel sample situated between two integer samples:

  • Horizontal Half-Pixels: The 6-tap filter is applied across six contiguous horizontal integer pixels (\(E, F, G, H, I, J\)). The half-pixel sample \(b\) between \(G\) and \(H\) is calculated as: \[b = \text{Clip}((E - 5F + 20G + 20H - 5I + J + 16) \gg 5)\]
  • Vertical Half-Pixels: The identical 6-tap operation is applied vertically along six contiguous integer pixels in a column to determine the vertical half-pixel values.

2. Diagonal Half-Pixel Interpolation

To determine the center half-pixel sample (surrounded diagonally by four integer samples):

  • The 6-tap filter is applied vertically to six previously calculated, unclipped horizontal half-pixel intermediate values (or horizontally to six vertical half-pixel intermediates).
  • The resulting value is normalized by dividing by 1024 (a 10-bit right shift) and clipping the outcome to the valid pixel range (e.g., 0–255 for 8-bit video).

3. Quarter-Pixel Interpolation

Once all half-pixel positions are computed, quarter-pixel samples are derived through simple bilinear interpolation:

  • Each quarter-pixel position is calculated by averaging the two nearest full-pixel or half-pixel neighbors using a 2-tap average filter: \[\text{Quarter-Pixel} = (P_1 + P_2 + 1) \gg 1\]
  • This two-tier approach keeps the computational overhead manageable by limiting the higher-order 6-tap calculation to the half-pixel grid.

Impact on Video Coding Efficiency

By applying the 6-tap Wiener filter, the encoder accurately reconstructs the underlying continuous signal of the video stream. This filtering prevents aliasing during frame transitions, reduces the energy of the residual signal sent to the transform stage, and ensures that sub-pixel motion compensation preserves sharp textures and fine spatial details across successive frames.
