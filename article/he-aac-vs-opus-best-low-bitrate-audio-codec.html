HE-AAC vs Opus: Best Low Bitrate Audio Codec

This article compares MPEG-4 High-Efficiency Advanced Audio Coding (HE-AAC) and the Opus audio codec at low bitrates, typically ranging from 16 kbps to 64 kbps. It examines how each codec handles bandwidth limitations, analyzes sound quality and compression artifacts, evaluates algorithmic latency, and reviews overall compatibility and licensing across modern streaming and communication platforms.

Architecture and Compression Techniques

HE-AAC and Opus rely on fundamentally different technological approaches to achieve high compression efficiency at low bitrates.

  • HE-AAC (v1 and v2): HE-AAC v1 builds on standard AAC-LC by adding Spectral Band Replication (SBR). SBR reconstructs higher audio frequencies at the decoder side using transposed lower frequencies and control data, saving significant bitrate. HE-AAC v2 introduces Parametric Stereo (PS), which encodes stereo audio as a mono downmix combined with small spatial parametric metadata, enabling intelligible stereo playback at bitrates as low as 16–32 kbps.
  • Opus: Opus is a hybrid codec that merges Skype’s SILK (optimized for human speech) and Xiph.Org’s CELT (optimized for low-latency music). Opus dynamically switches between or combines these modes depending on the audio type and available bandwidth. It does not rely on synthetic frequency reconstruction like SBR, instead using adaptive transform coding and linear prediction to preserve original audio features.

Audio Quality at Low Bitrates

  • Speech (8 kbps to 32 kbps): Opus significantly outperforms HE-AAC in speech clarity due to its dedicated SILK mode. Voices sound more natural and intelligible with fewer artifacts. HE-AAC can sound hollow or robotic at these ultra-low rates.
  • Music (32 kbps to 64 kbps): In music encoding, Opus consistently matches or exceeds HE-AAC v1/v2 in blind listening tests (such as MUSHRA tests). While HE-AAC v2 creates a convincing stereo image, the synthesized highs from SBR can introduce a distinct "metallic" or "swishy" artifact. Opus maintains a more cohesive frequency spectrum without the unnatural brightness typical of SBR.
  • Complex/Mixed Content: Opus adapts dynamically to mixed audio (such as gaming, podcasts with intro music, or video soundtracks), providing a smoother tonal transition than HE-AAC.

Latency and Real-Time Performance

  • Opus: Engineered from the ground up for interactive applications, Opus features an algorithmic delay ranging from 5 ms to 26.5 ms, with default settings around 20 ms. This makes it the industry standard for VoIP, real-time communications, and interactive live streaming (e.g., WebRTC, Discord).
  • HE-AAC: Because SBR and traditional AAC filter banks require larger lookahead buffers, HE-AAC typically introduces an algorithmic latency exceeding 100 ms. This delay makes it unsuitable for two-way real-time communication, confining its use primarily to one-way streaming and broadcasting.

Licensing, Ecosystem, and Hardware Support

  • Licensing: Opus is an open, royalty-free standard maintained by the Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF). HE-AAC is a proprietary standard governed by patent pools (such as Via Licensing), requiring royalty payments for hardware and commercial software implementations.
  • Hardware vs. Software Support: HE-AAC has extensive legacy support in dedicated hardware chips, digital radio standards (DAB+), digital television (DVB/ATSC), and older mobile devices. Opus is standard across all modern web browsers, operating systems, and internet-native streaming platforms (e.g., YouTube, Spotify).

Summary

At bitrates between 16 kbps and 64 kbps, Opus provides superior audio fidelity, lower latency, and zero licensing fees compared to HE-AAC. HE-AAC remains relevant primarily in traditional broadcast infrastructure and legacy hardware ecosystems where hardware-accelerated decoding is mandatory. For modern internet streaming, voice applications, and interactive media, Opus is the more capable and efficient codec.
