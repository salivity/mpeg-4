MPEG-4 Audio: Reflection and Diffraction in 3D

The MPEG-4 standard incorporates an advanced spatial audio engine within its Binary Format for Scenes (BIFS) that models realistic acoustic behaviors directly linked to 3D visual geometry. This article explains how the MPEG-4 spatial audio framework processes geometric data and material properties to compute real-time sound reflection, occlusion, and diffraction around physical barriers.

Geometric Scene Representation and Acoustic Properties

The MPEG-4 spatial audio engine relies on a synchronized scene graph where audio nodes interface with the 3D visual mesh. Rather than treating visual objects solely as graphical assets, the engine attaches acoustic properties to polygonal surfaces using dedicated scene description nodes (such as AcousticMaterial and PerceptualParameters).

Each 3D barrier in the scene is defined by geometric vertices and acoustic coefficients across different frequency bands:

  • Absorption Coefficients: Determine the fraction of sound energy absorbed by the material versus the fraction reflected.
  • Transmission Coefficients: Dictate how much sound energy penetrates through an occluding barrier.
  • Scattering/Diffusion Coefficients: Define the proportion of sound that is randomly scattered rather than specularly reflected.

Computing Sound Reflection

To compute sound reflections from walls and visual barriers, MPEG-4 combines deterministic geometric acoustics for early reflections with statistical models for late reverberation.

1. The Image Source Method for Early Reflections

For flat surfaces and low-order reflections, the engine uses the Image Source Method (ISM). The engine calculates the mirror image of a sound source relative to each reflective polygon by mirroring the source position across the plane of the polygon. If a valid, unblocked line of sight exists from the image source to the listener, a virtual sound source is instantiated. The engine then calculates:

  • The additional propagation delay based on the total path length.
  • High-frequency attenuation applied via low-order Infinite Impulse Response (IIR) or Finite Impulse Response (FIR) filters corresponding to the material's absorption profile.
  • Spatial positioning using Head-Related Transfer Functions (HRTFs) corresponding to the angle of arrival of the reflected ray.

2. Ray and Beam Tracing for Complex Environments

When geometries are too complex for exhaustive image-source calculations, the engine implements directional ray or beam tracing. Rays are cast from the source into the environment; upon hitting a barrier, new reflected paths are spawned based on Snell’s Law of reflection, taking the surface normal and scattering properties into account.

3. Late Reverberation

To maintain real-time performance, high-order reflections are not tracked individually. Instead, late diffuse reverberation is computed parametrically using Feedback Delay Networks (FDNs) configured by the collective volume, surface area, and average absorption of the surrounding enclosure.

Computing Sound Diffraction Around Barriers

When a visual barrier breaks the direct line-of-sight between a sound source and the listener, the direct path enters an acoustic "shadow zone." Sound waves physically bend around the edges of the barrier—a phenomenon known as diffraction. The MPEG-4 spatial audio engine computes diffraction using simplified formulations based on the Uniform Theory of Diffraction (UTD) and the Biot-Tolstoy-Medwin (BTM) technique.

1. Edge Detection and Shadow Zones

The engine analyzes the visual mesh to identify active diffracting edges (the convex boundaries of occluding polygons between the source and listener). It determines whether the listener is in the illuminated region, the transition zone, or the deep shadow zone of the obstacle.

2. Secondary Virtual Sources

For each active diffracting edge, the engine computes the shortest path that wraps from the source, touches the edge, and reaches the listener. The point on the edge where this occurs acts as a secondary virtual sound source:

  • Path-Length Delay: The total travel time is computed as the distance from the source to the edge point plus the distance from the edge point to the listener.
  • Angle of Deflection: The angle between the incident ray and the diffracted ray determines the attenuation level. Larger bending angles result in greater energy loss.

3. Frequency-Dependent Low-Pass Filtering

Because diffraction is inherently wavelength-dependent, low-frequency sounds (long wavelengths) bend around barriers far more effectively than high-frequency sounds (short wavelengths). The MPEG-4 engine applies a dynamic low-pass filter to the diffracted path. As the listener moves deeper into the shadow zone behind a barrier, the filter's cutoff frequency is progressively lowered, removing high frequencies and leaving only the muffled, diffracted low-frequency components.

Spatial Synthesis and Final Auralization

Once reflection paths and diffracted edge paths are computed, their respective signals are time-aligned, filtered according to distance, transmission, and diffraction formulas, and spatialized. The MPEG-4 rendering pipeline convolves each separate path with the listener's HRTF according to its precise angle of arrival, producing an accurate binaural or multichannel sound field that dynamically responds to movements around 3D visual barriers.
